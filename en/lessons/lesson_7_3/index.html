Why this matters

A clock shows 9. In 5 hours it will show 2, even though $9 + 5 = 14$. We constantly compute not with the objects themselves but with their “classes”: with the reading of the clock face instead of the number of hours elapsed, with the day of the week instead of the date, with the sign of a number instead of the number itself, with parity, a remainder, a checksum.

Such computation saves effort, but it hides a trap. The sign of a product can be found from the signs of the factors: “minus times minus gives plus.” But the sign of a sum cannot be found from the signs of the summands: $1 + (-1) = 0$, but $2 + (-5) = -3$. In the first case the operation can be transferred from numbers to their classes; in the second it cannot.

The property that distinguishes the first case from the second is called a congruence. This lesson works through the subsection “Congruence” of § 1 “Binary operations” in chapter three, “Algebras and algebraic systems,” of L. Ya. Kulikov's textbook “Algebra and Number Theory” (Moscow, 1979, in Russian; pp. 81–82): the definition of a congruence, Theorem 1.9 stating that a congruence defines an operation on the quotient set, and the definition of the associated operation.

We will prove everything rigorously, with the details the textbook leaves implicit, add the converse theorem, a convenient criterion and a view through “summaries,” and then work through 81 examples: 19 congruences from arithmetic and algebra, 15 counterexamples, 15 associated operations, 16 associations from everyday life and 16 applications — from checking multiplication by casting out nines and bank card numbers to cryptography and computing metrics in machine learning.

What to remember

A congruence links two notions: a binary operation and an equivalence relation. Let us recall both — they are covered in detail in and .

A binary operation on a set $A$ is a map $\top \colon A \times A \to A$. The image of a pair $\langle a, b\rangle$ is written $a \mathbin{\top} b$.

The definition hides two requirements: the result is defined for every pair and lies in $A$. Addition and multiplication on $\mathbb{Z}$ are binary operations. Subtraction on $\mathbb{N}$ is not: $3 - 5 \notin \mathbb{N}$. Division on $\mathbb{Z}$ is not either: $1 : 0$ is undefined, and $1 : 2 \notin \mathbb{Z}$.

A relation $R \subseteq A \times A$ is called an equivalence relation if it is reflexive ($a \,R\, a$), symmetric ($a \,R\, b$ implies $b \,R\, a$) and transitive ($a \,R\, b$ and $b \,R\, c$ imply $a \,R\, c$).

The equivalence class of an element $a$ is the set $a/R = \{x \in A \mid x \,R\, a\}$ of all elements equivalent to $a$. The set of all classes $A/R = \{a/R \mid a \in A\}$ is called the quotient set of the set $A$ by the relation $R$.

The key fact about classes on which the whole lesson rests:

$$a/R = c/R \iff a \,R\, c.$$

The classes do not overlap and cover all of $A$ — they split it into “boxes.” Any element of a class is called its representative. Instead of $a/R$ one often writes $[a]$ or $\bar a$; for remainders modulo $n$ we will write $[a]_n$.

Let $n \geqslant 1$ be a natural number. Integers $a$ and $b$ are congruent modulo $n$ if $a - b$ is divisible by $n$; one writes $a \equiv b \pmod{n}$. This is an equivalence: $a - a = 0$ is divisible by $n$; if $n \mid a - b$, then $n \mid b - a$; if $n \mid a - b$ and $n \mid b - c$, then $n \mid (a - b) + (b - c) = a - c$.

There are exactly $n$ classes — by the remainders $0, 1, \dots, n - 1$. For example, $[2]_{12} = \{\dots, -22, -10, 2, 14, 26, \dots\}$ — all the moments when the hour hand points to 2, if hours are counted from midnight of some day.

Intuition: computing with boxes

Suppose the elements of $A$ are sorted into boxes — classes. We would like to learn to “combine boxes”: from boxes $X$ and $Y$, obtain a box $X \mathbin{\top^*} Y$. Only one way suggests itself:

  1. take some element $a$ out of $X$ and some $b$ out of $Y$;
  2. compute $a \mathbin{\top} b$ — we do have the operation on the elements themselves;
  3. declare the answer to be the box that contains $a \mathbin{\top} b$.

The weak spot is the word “some.” If we take other elements $c$ and $d$ out of the same boxes, the result $c \mathbin{\top} d$ may land in a different box. Then the “product of boxes” is not defined: the answer depends on who did the picking.

A congruence is a guarantee that the choice of representatives does not matter. Whatever elements we take out of boxes $X$ and $Y$, the result will land in one and the same box. Then the operation on boxes can be computed through any representatives — and the answer will be unique.

Let us check this on a clock. The classes are clock-face readings, the operation is addition. Box “9” contains $9, 21, -3, \dots$; box “5” contains $5, 17, -7, \dots$ Let us pick different representatives:

$$9 + 5 = 14 \in [2]_{12}, \qquad 21 + 17 = 38 = 3 \cdot 12 + 2 \in [2]_{12}, \qquad -3 + (-7) = -10 \in [2]_{12}.$$

Every time it is box “2”: addition of clock readings is well defined. But with the boxes “negative,” “zero,” “positive” addition does not work: $1 + (-1) = 0$, while $2 + (-5) = -3$ — representatives of the same boxes gave answers in different ones.

The residue classes modulo $n$ are placed around a circle, as on a clock face. Choose $a$ and $b$: the blue and orange arrows show their classes, the black dot shows the class of $a \mathbin{\top} b$. The sliders $k$ and $m$ replace $a$ and $b$ with other representatives of the same classes, $c = a + kn$ and $d = b + mn$; the ring circles the class of $c \mathbin{\top} d$. For $+$, $-$ and $\cdot$ the ring is always on the dot. For $\max$ it is not: try $n = 2$, $a = 1$, $b = 2$, $k = 1$, $m = -1$.

Definition of a congruence

Let us translate the intuition into formulas. “$a$ and $c$ are from the same box” is $a \,R\, c$; “the results are in the same box” is $(a \mathbin{\top} b) \,R\, (c \mathbin{\top} d)$. Kulikov's definition:

Let $R$ be an equivalence relation on a set $A$ and $\top$ a binary operation on $A$. The relation $R$ is called a congruence with respect to the operation $\top$ if for any elements $a, b, c, d$ of the set $A$

$$a \,R\, c \;\;\text{and}\;\; b \,R\, d \quad\Longrightarrow\quad (a \mathbin{\top} b) \;R\; (c \mathbin{\top} d).$$
$a, c$
two representatives of one class — the “left arguments”
$b, d$
two representatives of one class — the “right arguments”
$a \mathbin{\top} b$, $c \mathbin{\top} d$
the results for two choices of representatives; they must land in the same class

In short: replace the arguments with equivalent ones — get an equivalent result. One also says that $R$ is compatible with the operation $\top$. The word “congruence” comes from the Latin congruens, “agreeing, corresponding.”

Let $a \equiv c$ and $b \equiv d \pmod n$, that is, $a - c = nk$ and $b - d = nl$ for some integers $k, l$. Then

$$(a + b) - (c + d) = (a - c) + (b - d) = n(k + l),$$
$$ab - cd = (a - c)\,b + c\,(b - d) = n(kb + cl).$$

Both differences are divisible by $n$, so $a + b \equiv c + d$ and $ab \equiv cd \pmod n$. The trick in the second line — subtract and add $cb$ — is worth remembering: it replaces the arguments one at a time.

On $\mathbb{Z}$ set $a \,R\, b \iff \operatorname{sgn} a = \operatorname{sgn} b$. There are three classes: the negative numbers, $\{0\}$, the positive numbers. For multiplication $\operatorname{sgn}(ab) = \operatorname{sgn} a \cdot \operatorname{sgn} b$, so $\operatorname{sgn} a = \operatorname{sgn} c$ and $\operatorname{sgn} b = \operatorname{sgn} d$ imply $\operatorname{sgn}(ab) = \operatorname{sgn}(cd)$ — a congruence. For addition a single bad quadruple suffices: $1 \,R\, 2$ and $(-1) \,R\, (-5)$, but $1 + (-1) = 0$ and $2 + (-5) = -3$ are not equivalent. Not a congruence.

The word comes from number theory. The sign $\equiv$ and the term “congruence” itself (congruentia) were introduced by C. F. Gauss in the “Disquisitiones Arithmeticae” (1801). The general notion of a congruence of an arbitrary algebra appeared in the 20th century, in G. Birkhoff's work on universal algebra (1935). In Kulikov's book it stands at the very beginning of the chapter on algebras: without it one can build neither the quotient algebra nor the homomorphism theorem.

Bone by bone

Formula has five parts, and each one matters.

“$R$ is an equivalence relation”

Without this there are no classes — and nothing to combine. A congruence is an equivalence with an extra property, not a new kind of relation. Reflexivity, symmetry and transitivity will still be needed in the proofs.

“for any $a, b, c, d$”

The property must hold for all quadruples. Hence the asymmetry between proof and refutation:

  • to prove a congruence you need a general argument — checking examples will not do, there are infinitely many quadruples;
  • to refute it, one quadruple — a counterexample — is enough.
“$a \,R\, c$ and $b \,R\, d$ imply”

Both arguments are replaced at once. Special cases: $c = a$ (by reflexivity $a \,R\, a$) — only the right argument changes; $d = b$ — only the left one.

Why both arguments matter

Take the operation $a \mathbin{\top} b = a^b$ on $\mathbb{N}$ and congruence modulo $3$. Replacing the base is safe: if $a \equiv c$, then $a^b \equiv c^b \pmod 3$ — these are $b$ multiplications in a row. But replacing the exponent is not: $1 \equiv 4 \pmod 3$, but $2^1 = 2$, while $2^4 = 16 \equiv 1 \pmod 3$. Half of the property is not enough.

“then $(a \mathbin{\top} b) \,R\, (c \mathbin{\top} d)$”

The results must be equivalent, not equal. If we required $a \mathbin{\top} b = c \mathbin{\top} d$, even the clock would fail: $9 + 5 = 14$ and $21 + 17 = 38$ are different numbers, but the same class modulo $12$.

“with respect to the operation $\top$”

A congruence is a property of a pair “relation + operation.” The same $R$ can be a congruence for one operation and fail to be one for another: the sign of a number — yes for multiplication, no for addition; congruence modulo $2$ — yes for $+$ and $\cdot$, no for $\max$ ($1 \equiv 3$ and $2 \equiv 0$, but $\max(1, 2) = 2$ is even, while $\max(3, 0) = 3$ is odd). The phrase “$R$ is a congruence” without naming the operation is meaningless.

Theorem 1.9: an operation on classes

Now the main point — the reason congruences are introduced at all. If $R$ is a congruence, then the “box rule” from the intuition defines a genuine binary operation on the quotient set.

Let $\top$ be a binary operation on a set $A$ and $R$ a congruence with respect to $\top$. Then the equality

$$(a/R) \mathbin{\top^*} (b/R) = (a \mathbin{\top} b)/R, \qquad a, b \in A,$$

defines a binary operation $\top^*$ on the quotient set $A/R$.

What is there to prove? Equality is a recipe: “to combine two classes, take representatives, combine them and take the class of the result.” The recipe becomes an operation only if it gives exactly one answer for every pair of classes. Kulikov says this in the language of sets: $\top^*$ is the set of pairs

$$\top^* = \Bigl\{\, \bigl\langle \langle a/R,\; b/R \rangle,\; (a \mathbin{\top} b)/R \bigr\rangle \;\Bigm|\; a, b \in A \,\Bigr\},$$

that is, a relation between $(A/R) \times (A/R)$ and $A/R$. We must prove that this relation is a function defined on all of $(A/R) \times (A/R)$.

Proof following Kulikov, with details

Step 1. The values lie in $A/R$. The second component of each pair in is the class $(a \mathbin{\top} b)/R$ of the element $a \mathbin{\top} b \in A$, that is, an element of $A/R$.

Step 2. Every pair of classes has a value. Let $X, Y \in A/R$. Classes are nonempty; choose $a \in X$ and $b \in Y$. Then $X = a/R$, $Y = b/R$, and the pair $\bigl\langle\langle X, Y\rangle, (a \mathbin{\top} b)/R\bigr\rangle$ lies in $\top^*$. (The textbook leaves this step implicit.)

Step 3. The value is unique — the heart of the theorem. Suppose $\top^*$ contains two pairs with the same “input”:

$$\bigl\langle\langle a/R, b/R\rangle, (a \mathbin{\top} b)/R\bigr\rangle \in \top^*, \qquad \bigl\langle\langle c/R, d/R\rangle, (c \mathbin{\top} d)/R\bigr\rangle \in \top^*, \qquad \langle a/R, b/R\rangle = \langle c/R, d/R\rangle.$$

We must show that the “outputs” are equal too: $(a \mathbin{\top} b)/R = (c \mathbin{\top} d)/R$. Ordered pairs are equal when their components are equal: $a/R = c/R$ and $b/R = d/R$. By this means $a \,R\, c$ and $b \,R\, d$. Since $R$ is a congruence, by we get $(a \mathbin{\top} b) \,R\, (c \mathbin{\top} d)$, and again by , $(a \mathbin{\top} b)/R = (c \mathbin{\top} d)/R$.

Thus $\top^*$ assigns to each pair of classes exactly one class — it is a binary operation on the quotient set $A/R$.

The converse is also true: if the recipe is well defined, then $R$ must be a congruence. So a congruence is not merely a sufficient but a necessary and sufficient condition for the operation to be transferable to classes.

Let $R$ be an equivalence on $A$ such that the class $(a \mathbin{\top} b)/R$ depends only on the classes $a/R$ and $b/R$, not on the choice of $a$ and $b$ in them. Then $R$ is a congruence with respect to $\top$.

Proof

Let $a \,R\, c$ and $b \,R\, d$. By $a/R = c/R$ and $b/R = d/R$: the elements $a, c$ are representatives of one class, $b, d$ of another. By hypothesis the class of the result does not depend on the choice of representatives: $(a \mathbin{\top} b)/R = (c \mathbin{\top} d)/R$, that is, $(a \mathbin{\top} b) \,R\, (c \mathbin{\top} d)$.

The binary operation $\top^*$ defined on the quotient set $A/R$ by equality is called the operation associated with the operation $\top$ by the congruence $R$.

Other books call it the induced or quotient operation, and the pair $(A/R, \top^*)$ a quotient algebra. In words: the associated operation is the same operation $\top$ seen “up to $R$,” when the differences inside a class are no longer visible.

The map $\pi \colon A \to A/R$, $\pi(a) = a/R$ (“put an element into its box”), is called the natural map. In its terms reads $\pi(a \mathbin{\top} b) = \pi(a) \mathbin{\top^*} \pi(b)$: it makes no difference whether we first compute and then take the class or first take the classes and then compute. Maps with this property are homomorphisms; they are the subject of .

Congruence modulo $4$ is a congruence for $+$ and $\cdot$; there are four classes. At the intersection of row $[a]$ and column $[b]$ stands the class of the result; we compute with any representatives: $[3] + [2] = [5] = [1]$, $[3] \cdot [2] = [6] = [2]$, and with the representatives $-1 \in [3]$ and $10 \in [2]$: $-1 + 10 = 9 \in [1]$, $(-1) \cdot 10 = -10 \in [2]$ — the same thing.

How to check a congruence

Checking with four variables at once is inconvenient. Transitivity lets us change the arguments one at a time.

An equivalence $R$ is a congruence with respect to $\top$ if and only if for any $a, b, c \in A$

$$a \,R\, c \;\Longrightarrow\; (a \mathbin{\top} b) \,R\, (c \mathbin{\top} b) \;\;\text{and}\;\; (b \mathbin{\top} a) \,R\, (b \mathbin{\top} c).$$
Proof

$(\Rightarrow)$ Let $a \,R\, c$. By reflexivity $b \,R\, b$. Applying to the pairs “$a \,R\, c$, $b \,R\, b$” and “$b \,R\, b$, $a \,R\, c$,” we obtain both relations.

$(\Leftarrow)$ Let $a \,R\, c$ and $b \,R\, d$. We change first the left argument, then the right one:

$$(a \mathbin{\top} b) \;R\; (c \mathbin{\top} b) \;R\; (c \mathbin{\top} d).$$

By transitivity $(a \mathbin{\top} b) \,R\, (c \mathbin{\top} d)$.

For a commutative operation ($a \mathbin{\top} b = b \mathbin{\top} a$) one of the two conditions is enough. For a noncommutative one both are needed: the example with the power $a^b$ from the breakdown shows that one can hold while the other fails.

Recipe. To prove a congruence, take $a \,R\, c$, write out the equivalence by definition (for example, $a - c = nk$) and show that $a \mathbin{\top} b$ and $c \mathbin{\top} b$ (and, for a noncommutative operation, also $b \mathbin{\top} a$ and $b \mathbin{\top} c$) are equivalent. To refute it, look for a counterexample among small numbers, starting with “borderline” representatives: zero, one, numbers of different signs, the ends of a class.

Congruence as a “sufficient summary”

Almost all equivalences in practice are built the same way. An object has a “summary” — a remainder, a sign, a length, a checksum, a mean — and objects are equivalent when their summaries coincide. For such relations Theorem 1.9 turns into a very practical question.

Let $f \colon A \to B$ be any map and $a \,R_f\, c \iff f(a) = f(c)$. Then $R_f$ is an equivalence relation (it is called the kernel of the map $f$), and $R_f$ is a congruence with respect to $\top$ if and only if there is an operation $\circ$ on the set $f(A)$ for which

$$f(a \mathbin{\top} b) = f(a) \circ f(b) \qquad \text{for all } a, b \in A.$$
Proof

Reflexivity, symmetry and transitivity of $R_f$ are inherited from equality.

$(\Leftarrow)$ If $f(a) = f(c)$ and $f(b) = f(d)$, then $f(a \mathbin{\top} b) = f(a) \circ f(b) = f(c) \circ f(d) = f(c \mathbin{\top} d)$.

$(\Rightarrow)$ For $u, v \in f(A)$ choose $a, b$ with $f(a) = u$, $f(b) = v$ and set $u \circ v = f(a \mathbin{\top} b)$. Another choice $c, d$ gives $a \,R_f\, c$ and $b \,R_f\, d$, and by the congruence property $f(c \mathbin{\top} d) = f(a \mathbin{\top} b)$ — the definition is well posed; this is the same argument as in Theorem 1.9. Equality holds by construction.

In plain words: $R_f$ is a congruence when the summary of the result can be computed from the summaries of the arguments, without looking at the arguments themselves.

  • The remainder of a sum from the remainders of the summands: yes. The remainder of a product from the remainders of the factors: yes.
  • The sign of a product from the signs of the factors: yes. The sign of a sum: no.
  • The length of a concatenated word from the lengths of its parts: yes, $|uv| = |u| + |v|$.
  • The determinant of a product of matrices from the determinants: yes, $\det(AB) = \det A \cdot \det B$; the determinant of a sum: no. Matrices and the determinant are covered in .

Let $A$ be the finite collections of numbers (with repetitions), the operation the merging of collections $\uplus$, the summary $f$ the mean. Take $X = \{2, 4\}$ and $X' = \{3\}$ (mean $3$), $Y = \{6\}$ and $Y' = \{6, 6\}$ (mean $6$). Then

$$\operatorname{mean}(X \uplus Y) = \tfrac{2 + 4 + 6}{3} = 4, \qquad \operatorname{mean}(X' \uplus Y') = \tfrac{3 + 6 + 6}{3} = 5.$$

“Same mean” is not a congruence for merging: the mean of a union cannot be found from the means of the parts. Let us take a richer summary — the pair $g(X) = (\text{sum}, \text{count})$. It works: $g(X \uplus Y) = g(X) + g(Y)$ componentwise. And the mean is obtained from it at the very end: the sum divided by the count.

This is everyday practice in machine learning. The accuracy of a model is computed over batches. If $90$ answers out of $100$ are correct in the first batch and $1$ out of $10$ in the second, then the average of the two accuracies is $\tfrac{0.9 + 0.1}{2} = 0.5$, while the true accuracy is $\tfrac{91}{110} \approx 0.827$. That is why metrics in distributed training accumulate sums and counts and divide only at the very end. Such summaries are called mergeable summaries — they are exactly the congruences for the operation “merge the data.”

What the associated operation inherits

Let $R$ be a congruence with respect to $\top$. Then:

  1. if $\top$ is commutative, then $\top^*$ is commutative too;
  2. if $\top$ is associative, then $\top^*$ is associative too;
  3. if $e$ is an identity element for $\top$, then $e/R$ is an identity element for $\top^*$;
  4. if $a'$ is symmetric (inverse) to $a$ ($a \mathbin{\top} a' = a' \mathbin{\top} a = e$), then $a'/R$ is symmetric to $a/R$.
Proof

Everything follows from : we move the computation to representatives, use the property of $\top$ and return to classes. Associativity:

$$\bigl(a/R \mathbin{\top^*} b/R\bigr) \mathbin{\top^*} c/R = \bigl((a \mathbin{\top} b) \mathbin{\top} c\bigr)/R = \bigl(a \mathbin{\top} (b \mathbin{\top} c)\bigr)/R = a/R \mathbin{\top^*} \bigl(b/R \mathbin{\top^*} c/R\bigr).$$

The rest goes the same way: $a/R \mathbin{\top^*} b/R = (a \mathbin{\top} b)/R = (b \mathbin{\top} a)/R = b/R \mathbin{\top^*} a/R$; $\;a/R \mathbin{\top^*} e/R = (a \mathbin{\top} e)/R = a/R$; $\;a/R \mathbin{\top^*} a'/R = (a \mathbin{\top} a')/R = e/R$ (and symmetrically in the last two).

The converse is false: the associated operation may acquire properties that the original one did not have.

  • Concatenation of words is noncommutative: “sun” + “set” = “sunset,” but “set” + “sun” = “setsun.” Yet up to “same length” it turns into addition of lengths — which is commutative.
  • In $(\mathbb{Z}, \cdot)$ the number $3$ has no inverse, but in $\mathbb{Z}/4$ the class $[3]$ is invertible: $[3] \cdot [3] = [9] = [1]$.

The associated operation is a simplified copy of the original: everything that was true stays true, and some new things become true thanks to the gluing.

Examples of congruences: arithmetic and algebra

Nineteen congruences — from school arithmetic to the construction of number systems. Each has a short proof; following the pattern of , it can be expanded into a full one.

  1. Addition modulo $n$ on $\mathbb{Z}$.

    $(a + b) - (c + d) = (a - c) + (b - d)$ — a sum of two multiples of $n$. On classes it is “clock” addition: $[9]_{12} + [5]_{12} = [2]_{12}$.

  2. Multiplication modulo $n$.

    $ab - cd = (a - c)\,b + c\,(b - d)$. For example, $[5]_7 \cdot [4]_7 = [20]_7 = [6]_7$; with the representatives $-2$ and $11$: $-22 = -4 \cdot 7 + 6$ — the same class.

  3. Subtraction modulo $n$.

    $(a - b) - (c - d) = (a - c) - (b - d)$. That is why “how many hours from 22:00 to 3:00” is $[3 - 22]_{24} = [-19]_{24} = [5]_{24}$.

  4. Any “polynomial” expression modulo $n$.

    $a \mathbin{\top} b = 3a + 5b$, $a^2 + ab$ and any expression built from additions and multiplications with integer coefficients: each step respects the congruence, and hence so does the whole chain. The practical conclusion: the remainder may be taken at any intermediate step.

  5. The sign of a number for multiplication on $\mathbb{Z}$.

    $\operatorname{sgn}(ab) = \operatorname{sgn} a \cdot \operatorname{sgn} b$ — the summary of the result is computed from the summaries of the arguments.

  6. The sign of a number for $\max$ and $\min$.

    The function $\operatorname{sgn}$ is nondecreasing, so $\operatorname{sgn} \max(a, b) = \max(\operatorname{sgn} a, \operatorname{sgn} b)$, and likewise for $\min$.

  7. “Same decade” $\lfloor a/10 \rfloor$ for $\max$ and $\min$.

    The same argument: $a \mapsto \lfloor a/10 \rfloor$ is nondecreasing. In general, the kernel of any nondecreasing function is a congruence for $\max$ and $\min$.

  8. The absolute value for multiplication on $\mathbb{R} \setminus \{0\}$.

    $|ab| = |a| \cdot |b|$. The classes are $\{-x, x\}$; the associated operation is multiplication of positive numbers.

  9. The fractional part for addition on $\mathbb{R}$.

    $a \,R\, b \iff a - b \in \mathbb{Z}$; $(a + b) - (c + d) = (a - c) + (b - d) \in \mathbb{Z}$. The classes are the points of a circle of length $1$.

  10. The last $k$ bits for bitwise AND, OR, XOR.

    The $k$ low-order bits of the result depend only on the $k$ low-order bits of the arguments. This is congruence modulo $2^k$ on the nonnegative integers.

  11. The length of a word for gluing (concatenation).

    $|uv| = |u| + |v|$.

  12. The first letter of a word for concatenation.

    The empty word is a separate class. If $u$ is nonempty, $uv$ starts with the first letter of $u$; if $u$ is empty, then $u'$ is empty too, and $uv = v$, $u'v' = v'$. A congruence — and a rare case of a noncommutative associated operation.

  13. The determinant for multiplication of $n \times n$ matrices.

    $\det(AB) = \det A \cdot \det B$, so $\det A = \det A'$ and $\det B = \det B'$ imply $\det(AB) = \det(A'B')$.

  14. The value of a polynomial at a point for $+$ and $\cdot$.

    On $\mathbb{R}[x]$: $p \,R\, q \iff p(2) = q(2)$. Substitution respects both operations: $(p + q)(2) = p(2) + q(2)$, $(pq)(2) = p(2)\,q(2)$.

  15. Polynomials modulo $x^2 + 1$.

    $p \,R\, q \iff p - q$ is divisible by $x^2 + 1$. The proof is word for word the same as for numbers: $pq - p'q' = (p - p')\,q + p'\,(q - q')$.

  16. “The view through a window” on subsets.

    Fix $M \subseteq U$; $X \,R\, Y \iff X \cap M = Y \cap M$. Then $(X \cup Y) \cap M = (X \cap M) \cup (Y \cap M)$, and likewise for $\cap$: a congruence for union and intersection.

  17. Two trivial congruences.

    Equality (each class is one element) and “everything is equivalent to everything” (one class) are congruences for any operation. All the others lie between them.

  18. Pairs of natural numbers — the integers.

    $(a, b) \sim (c, d) \iff a + d = b + c$, with componentwise addition. The pair $(a, b)$ stands for the “difference $a - b$,” which cannot always be computed in $\mathbb{N}$. From $a + d = b + c$ and $a' + d' = b' + c'$ it follows that $(a + a') + (d + d') = (b + b') + (c + c')$.

  19. Pairs of integers — fractions.

    Pairs $(a, b)$ with $b \ne 0$, $(a, b) \sim (c, d) \iff ad = bc$; addition $(a, b) \oplus (c, d) = (ad + bc,\, bd)$ and multiplication $(ac,\, bd)$. Both operations are compatible with $\sim$ (the check is Problem 6); this is how $\mathbb{Q}$ is built.

Counterexamples: where there is no congruence

Fifteen equivalence relations that are not compatible with the operation. Each has a concrete quadruple: $a \,R\, c$, $b \,R\, d$, but $a \mathbin{\top} b$ and $c \mathbin{\top} d$ are in different classes.

  1. Sign and addition.

    $1 \,R\, 2$, $(-1) \,R\, (-5)$, but $1 + (-1) = 0$, while $2 + (-5) = -3$.

  2. Sign and subtraction.

    $1 \,R\, 1$, $1 \,R\, 2$, but $1 - 1 = 0$, while $1 - 2 = -1$.

  3. Absolute value and addition.

    $1 \,R\, (-1)$ (both have $|\cdot| = 1$), $1 \,R\, 1$, but $1 + 1 = 2$, while $-1 + 1 = 0$. For multiplication the same relation is a congruence (example 8 above).

  4. Parity and $\max$.

    $1 \equiv 3$, $2 \equiv 0 \pmod 2$, but $\max(1, 2) = 2$ is even, while $\max(3, 0) = 3$ is odd.

  5. Congruence modulo $3$ in the exponent.

    $2 \equiv 2$, $1 \equiv 4 \pmod 3$, but $2^1 = 2$, while $2^4 = 16 \equiv 1 \pmod 3$. The base may be replaced, the exponent may not.

  6. Congruence modulo $3$ and the gcd.

    $1 \equiv 4$, $2 \equiv 2 \pmod 3$, but $\gcd(1, 2) = 1$, while $\gcd(4, 2) = 2$, and $1 \not\equiv 2 \pmod 3$.

  7. Number of digits and addition.

    $1 \,R\, 9$, $1 \,R\, 1$, but $1 + 1 = 2$ (one digit), while $9 + 1 = 10$ (two).

  8. Rounding to an integer and addition.

    $1.6$ and $2.4$ both round to $2$, but $1.6 + 1.6 = 3.2 \to 3$, while $2.4 + 2.4 = 4.8 \to 5$. Hence “the sum of the rounded values is not the rounded sum.”

  9. Fractional part and multiplication.

    $0.5 \,R\, 1.5$, $0.5 \,R\, 0.5$, but $0.5 \cdot 0.5 = 0.25$, while $1.5 \cdot 0.5 = 0.75$. For addition the same equivalence is a congruence (example 9).

  10. Determinant and addition of $2 \times 2$ matrices.

    $\det I = \det(-I) = 1$, so $I \,R\, (-I)$; and $I \,R\, I$. But $\det(I + I) = \det(2I) = 4$, while $\det(-I + I) = \det 0 = 0$.

  11. Degree of a polynomial and addition.

    $x \,R\, x$ and $x \,R\, (-x + 1)$ (both of degree $1$), but $x + x = 2x$ has degree $1$, while $x + (-x + 1) = 1$ has degree $0$. For multiplication of nonzero polynomials “same degree” is a congruence: $\deg(pq) = \deg p + \deg q$.

  12. Number of elements and union of sets.

    $\{1\} \,R\, \{2\}$, $\{1\} \,R\, \{1\}$, but $|\{1\} \cup \{1\}| = 1$, while $|\{2\} \cup \{1\}| = 2$.

  13. “Palindrome or not” and concatenation of words.

    “a” and “b” are both palindromes, “a” $R$ “a,” but “aa” is a palindrome, while “ba” is not.

  14. Fractions and “adding numerators and denominators.”

    The operation $(a, b) \oplus (c, d) = (a + c,\, b + d)$ (the mediant). $\tfrac12 = \tfrac24$, but $\tfrac12 \oplus \tfrac13 = \tfrac25$, while $\tfrac24 \oplus \tfrac13 = \tfrac37$, and $\tfrac25 \ne \tfrac37$ ($2 \cdot 7 \ne 3 \cdot 5$). This is why fractions cannot be added this way: the result depends on how the fraction is written.

  15. The mean and merging collections.

    $\{2, 4\} \,R\, \{3\}$, $\{6\} \,R\, \{6, 6\}$, but the mean of $\{2, 4, 6\}$ is $4$, while the mean of $\{3, 6, 6\}$ is $5$.

A gallery of associated operations

Fifteen quotient algebras: what we get when an operation is transferred to classes by Theorem 1.9.

  1. $\mathbb{Z}/2$, addition — “exclusive or.”

    The classes “even” and “odd”: even + even = even, even + odd = odd, odd + odd = even. If even is $0$ and odd is $1$, this is XOR.

  2. $\mathbb{Z}/2$, multiplication — “and.”

    A product is odd only if both factors are odd: logical AND.

  3. Signs with multiplication — the rule of signs.

    $(-) \cdot (-) = (+)$, $(-) \cdot (+) = (-)$, $(+) \cdot (+) = (+)$, zero absorbs everything. The school rule “minus times minus gives plus” is the table of an associated operation.

  4. $\mathbb{Z}/12$ — the clock face.

    $[9] + [5] = [2]$, $[11] + [11] = [10]$. The identity class is $[0]$; the opposite of $[5]$ is the class $[7]$.

  5. $\mathbb{Z}/7$ — the days of the week.

    If Monday is $[0]$, then Friday is $[4]$, and $[4] + [10] = [14] = [0]$: 10 days after Friday it is Monday.

  6. $\mathbb{Z}/4$ with multiplication — zero divisors.

    $[2] \cdot [2] = [0]$: a product of nonzero classes can be zero (the table above).

  7. $\mathbb{Z}/5$ with multiplication — every nonzero class has an inverse.

    $[2] \cdot [3] = [6] = [1]$, $[4] \cdot [4] = [16] = [1]$. Division by nonzero classes is possible — we get a field.

  8. $\mathbb{R}$ modulo $\mathbb{Z}$ — addition on a circle.

    Add the fractional parts and discard the integer part: $\{0.7\} + \{0.6\} = \{1.3\} = \{0.3\}$. This is how phases of oscillations and fractions of a turn add up.

  9. Words by length — natural numbers with addition.

    The class of a word is its length; concatenation of classes is addition of lengths: $(\mathbb{N}_0, +)$.

  10. Words by first letter — “the left one wins.”

    $X \mathbin{\top^*} Y = X$ if $X$ is not the class of the empty word, and $Y$ otherwise. The operation is associative but noncommutative: $[\text{a}] \mathbin{\top^*} [\text{b}] = [\text{a}]$, while $[\text{b}] \mathbin{\top^*} [\text{a}] = [\text{b}]$.

  11. Matrices by determinant — numbers with multiplication.

    The class of a matrix is its determinant; the product of classes is the product of numbers: the set $\mathbb{R}$ with multiplication.

  12. Polynomials modulo $x^2 + 1$ — the complex numbers.

    Each class contains exactly one polynomial of the form $a + bx$, and $x^2 \equiv -1$. For example, $(1 + 2x)(3 - x) = 3 + 5x - 2x^2 \equiv 5 + 5x$ — exactly like $(1 + 2i)(3 - i) = 5 + 5i$.

  13. Pairs of naturals — the integers.

    $(2, 5)$ and $(0, 3)$ stand for $-3$, the pair $(4, 1)$ for the number $3$; $(2, 5) + (4, 1) = (6, 6)$ — that is $0$, as it should be: $-3 + 3 = 0$.

  14. Pairs of integers — the rational numbers.

    $(1, 2) \oplus (1, 3) = (5, 6)$ and $(2, 4) \oplus (1, 3) = (10, 12)$ — one and the same fraction $\tfrac56$, because $10 \cdot 6 = 12 \cdot 5$.

  15. Subsets through a window $M$ — the subsets of $M$.

    The class of a set $X$ is what is visible in the window, $X \cap M$; union and intersection of classes are union and intersection of these parts: the algebra of sets $\mathcal{P}(M)$.

Associations from everyday life

Sixteen everyday situations. Each has a set, an operation and an equivalence “the same for our purposes.” The question is always the same: can we combine “the same” things and get “the same” results?

  1. Clocks congruence

    Time in hours, the operation “add,” the equivalence — the same clock-face reading (modulo $12$). 9 o'clock + 5 hours → 2 o'clock, whatever the day. You can plan by the clock face without remembering the date.

  2. Days of the week congruence

    Day numbers modulo $7$. Friday + 10 days = Monday, because $10 \equiv 3 \pmod 7$. Friday + 100 days = Sunday: $100 = 14 \cdot 7 + 2$.

  3. Months congruence

    Modulo $12$, with January as $0$. March + 11 months: $2 + 11 = 13 \equiv 1$ — February of the next year.

  4. A ship's heading congruence

    Angles modulo $360^\circ$. A heading of $270^\circ$ (west) plus a turn of $180^\circ$ gives $450^\circ \equiv 90^\circ$ — east. Turns add up by classes.

  5. A light switch congruence

    The number of presses modulo $2$. One person pressed 7 times, another 4 times — the outcome is as from a single press: $7 + 4 = 11 \equiv 1$. The light cares only about parity.

  6. Eggs in cartons of 10 congruence

    How many eggs will be left without a carton depends only on the remainders of the batches: $23$ and $18$ give $3 + 8 = 11 \to 1$, and indeed $23 + 18 = 41$.

  7. A chess knight congruence

    The color of the square $(x, y)$ is the parity of $x + y$. A knight's move changes $x + y$ by an odd number ($\pm1 \pm 2$), so every move changes the color. A conclusion without any search: after an odd number of moves the knight cannot return to its square.

  8. Musical notes congruence

    Pitch in semitones, the equivalence — the same note name (modulo $12$, the octave does not matter). C + 7 semitones = G in any octave: $60 + 7 = 67$, $48 + 7 = 55$, and $67 \equiv 55 \equiv 7 \pmod{12}$.

  9. A wallet congruence

    Sets of banknotes, the operation — put them into one wallet, the equivalence — the same total. The total of the union equals the sum of the totals, so the cashier does not care which banknotes you pay with.

  10. Profit and loss for · for +

    The income of a trade is the price change times the position size. The price fell ($-$) and the position is short ($-$) — the income is positive: the sign of a product is known from the signs. But “profit of one trade + loss of another = ?” depends on the amounts: for addition, the sign is not a congruence.

  11. Mixing solutions not a congruence

    The equivalence — the same concentration, the operation — pour together. $100$ g of a 10% solution + $100$ g of a 30% solution give $20\,\%$, while $300$ g of a 10% solution + $100$ g of a 30% solution give $15\,\%$. A congruent summary is the pair (mass of salt, mass of solution).

  12. The average score of two school classes not a congruence

    The average score of the combined class is not determined by the average scores of its parts — the sizes of the classes are needed too. This is the same example with the mean and merging.

  13. Rounded percentages not a congruence

    $33.3\,\% + 33.3\,\% + 33.4\,\% = 100\,\%$, but after rounding $33 + 33 + 33 = 99$. Rounding is not compatible with addition — that is why percentages in reports “don't add up.”

  14. Letter grades not a congruence

    Scores 85–100 earn an “A,” 70–84 a “B.” The average of $85$ and $70$ is $77.5$ — a “B,” while the average of $100$ and $84$ is $92$ — an “A.” From two grades you cannot tell the grade for the average.

  15. Age groups for + for max

    “Twentysomethings” are people from 20 to 29 years old. In 5 years a 25-year-old becomes a thirtysomething, while a 21-year-old stays in the same group: addition does not respect the groups. But the group of the older of the two is determined by the groups: $\max$ is compatible with “decades.”

  16. The Rubik's Cube congruence

    The twist of each corner is $0$, $1$ or $2$ thirds of a turn, and any face turn changes the total twist by a multiple of $3$. Computing this total in $\mathbb{Z}/3$ is legitimate because congruence modulo $3$ is a congruence for addition. The conclusion: if you twist a single corner of a solved cube, it can no longer be solved by face turns.

Where this works

Sixteen applications. Almost everywhere the same consequence of Theorem 1.9 is used: the class may be taken at any intermediate step — and you can compute with small numbers instead of huge ones.

  1. Checking multiplication by casting out nines

    A number is congruent modulo $9$ to the sum of its digits ($10 \equiv 1$). Let us check $47 \cdot 38 = 1786$: $47 \to 4 + 7 = 11 \to 2$, $38 \to 11 \to 2$, $2 \cdot 2 = 4$; $1786 \to 1 + 7 + 8 + 6 = 22 \to 4$. It checks out. Accountants have used this for centuries. A limitation: the check does not notice a transposition of digits — $1768$ also gives $4$.

  2. The remainder of a huge number — Horner's scheme

    $123456 \bmod 7$, digit by digit: $r \leftarrow (10r + \text{digit}) \bmod 7$ gives $1, 5, 4, 2, 4, 4$. The answer is $4$ — and not a single number larger than $70$. This is how an IBAN is checked: an account number with 30-odd digits must leave remainder $1$ when divided by $97$, and it is divided in chunks.

  3. Check digits

    ISBN-10 — the weighted sum of the digits is divisible by $11$; the EAN-13 barcode — the sum with weights $1$ and $3$ is divisible by $10$; a bank card number — the Luhn algorithm modulo $10$; the INN (the Russian taxpayer number) — weighted sums modulo $11$, then $10$. A typo in one digit changes the class of the sum — and the number fails the check.

  4. Integers in a processor

    Unsigned 32-bit numbers are added and multiplied modulo $2^{32}$: on overflow the high-order bits are simply lost. The result is still correct “modulo,” because the relation “≡” is a congruence; the compiler may reorder and regroup such operations, since associativity and commutativity are inherited.

  5. Pseudorandom number generators

    The linear congruential generator $x_{t+1} = (a x_t + c) \bmod m$ — the name speaks for itself. The Mulberry32 generator in the sister course “Gradient Boosting, bone by bone” also works in arithmetic modulo $2^{32}$ — that is why the browser and Python produce the same numbers.

  6. Hash tables and substring search

    The polynomial hash of a string $h = (s_0 p^{k-1} + \dots + s_{k-1}) \bmod q$ is updated in one step when the window shifts (the Rabin–Karp algorithm): subtract the first character, multiply, add the new one — everything is reduced modulo the same number, so the intermediate numbers stay small.

  7. Cryptography

    RSA and the Diffie–Hellman protocol raise numbers with hundreds of digits to huge powers modulo a number. This is possible only because the remainder is taken after every multiplication: otherwise the intermediate numbers would not fit into any computer.

  8. CRC and Reed–Solomon codes

    The CRC checksum in archives and network packets is the remainder of dividing the message polynomial by a generator polynomial (coefficients modulo $2$). Congruence of polynomials modulo a polynomial is a congruence, so CRC is computed byte by byte with a table. Recovery of data from a scratched disc and a QR code rests on the same idea.

  9. The parity bit and RAID

    A parity bit is the sum of the bits in $\mathbb{Z}/2$, that is, XOR. In a RAID-5 array the parity block makes it possible to recover any single lost disk: the missing summand is found by subtraction, and in $\mathbb{Z}/2$ subtraction coincides with addition.

  10. Calendar algorithms

    The day of the week for a date (Zeller's congruence, Conway's Doomsday rule) is computed in $\mathbb{Z}/7$: the shifts for years, months and days are added directly modulo $7$.

  11. Angles in navigation, robotics and games

    Directions are classes modulo $360^\circ$ (or $2\pi$). The total rotation of a robot or a camera is computed by classes; a programmer “wraps” the angle into $[0^\circ, 360^\circ)$ at every step without fear of error.

  12. Cyclic features in machine learning

    The hour of the day is an element of $\mathbb{Z}/24$: 23:00 and 0:00 are neighbors. The feature is encoded by the pair $\bigl(\cos\tfrac{2\pi h}{24}, \sin\tfrac{2\pi h}{24}\bigr)$: the kernel of this map is exactly congruence modulo $24$, and adding a duration corresponds to rotating the point on the circle.

  13. Mergeable summaries and distributed computing

    Accuracy, the mean and the variance are computed on chunks of data and then merged. What must be stored is a congruent summary: for the mean — (sum, count), for the variance — (count, $\sum x$, $\sum x^2$). The mean of means is a common mistake: $0.5$ instead of $0.827$ in the example above.

  14. Case-insensitive search

    The relation “equal ignoring case” is compatible with concatenation of strings: $\operatorname{lower}(uv) = \operatorname{lower}(u)\operatorname{lower}(v)$ — that is why text can be lowercased in chunks. A subtlety: Unicode has exceptions — the Greek sigma at the end of a word is written differently ($\varsigma$), and the character-by-character rule breaks.

  15. The 15 puzzle and the parity of permutations

    The parity of a permutation is compatible with composition: even after even is even, odd after odd is even too (the associated operation is $\mathbb{Z}/2$). Every move in the 15 puzzle is a transposition with the empty square: it changes both the parity of the permutation and the parity of the position of the empty square, so their sum in $\mathbb{Z}/2$ does not change. For the position “14 and 15 swapped” this sum is different — it cannot be solved.

  16. Mathematics itself: building number systems

    The integers are classes of pairs of naturals, the rationals are classes of pairs of integers, the complex numbers are classes of polynomials modulo $x^2 + 1$; the real numbers are built from sequences in the same way. Each time the operations are defined on representatives, and each time Theorem 1.9 is needed: without checking the congruence we would get the “mediant” instead of addition of fractions.

Common mistakes

For $\max$ and parity: $\max(1, 0) = 1$ and $\max(3, 0) = 3$ — both odd, “everything checks out.” But $\max(1, 2) = 2$ and $\max(3, 0) = 3$ do not. Examples can only refute; a proof is a general argument.

The definition says $(a \mathbin{\top} b) \,R\, (c \mathbin{\top} d)$, not $a \mathbin{\top} b = c \mathbin{\top} d$. $9 + 5 \ne 21 + 17$, but the classes modulo $12$ coincide — and that is enough.

“The sign of a number is a congruence” is an incomplete statement. For multiplication — yes, for addition — no. A congruence is always “with respect to” a specific operation.

A classic student mistake: $\tfrac{a}{b} + \tfrac{c}{d} = \tfrac{a + c}{b + d}$. Such an “operation” depends on how the fraction is written: $\tfrac12 + \tfrac13$ “equals” $\tfrac25$, while $\tfrac24 + \tfrac13$ “equals” $\tfrac37$. Any rule of the form “take a representative and compute” is first checked for well-definedness.

$2 \cdot 1 \equiv 2 \cdot 3 \pmod 4$, but $1 \not\equiv 3 \pmod 4$. Division is not an operation on $\mathbb{Z}$, and Theorem 1.9 says nothing about it; in $\mathbb{Z}/4$ the equation $[2] \cdot x = [1]$ has no solutions.

$2^{4} \bmod 3 \ne 2^{4 \bmod 3} \bmod 3$: $16 \equiv 1$, while $2^1 = 2$. For $a^b$ the congruence is compatible with the base but not with the exponent.

Problems

Find $[4]_6 + [5]_6$, $[4]_6 \cdot [5]_6$ and $[3]_6 \cdot [4]_6$. Check the first two answers with other representatives: $10 \in [4]_6$ and $-1 \in [5]_6$.

Solution

$4 + 5 = 9 \to [3]_6$; $4 \cdot 5 = 20 = 3 \cdot 6 + 2 \to [2]_6$; $3 \cdot 4 = 12 \to [0]_6$ — another pair of zero divisors.

With other representatives: $10 + (-1) = 9 \to [3]_6$, $10 \cdot (-1) = -10 = -2 \cdot 6 + 2 \to [2]_6$. They coincide, as Theorem 1.9 promises.

On the nonnegative integers let $a \,R\, b$ if $a$ and $b$ have the same last digit. Is $R$ a congruence with respect to addition? multiplication? $\max$?

Solution

The last digit is the remainder modulo $10$, so for addition and multiplication — yes (examples 1 and 2). For $\max$ — no: $1 \,R\, 11$, $5 \,R\, 5$, but $\max(1, 5) = 5$, while $\max(11, 5) = 11$ — the last digits are $5$ and $1$.

Prove that the relation “same sign” on $\mathbb{Z}$ is a congruence with respect to multiplication, and write the table of the associated operation on the three classes $\{-, 0, +\}$. Which class is the identity? Which classes have a symmetric (inverse) element?

Solution

$\operatorname{sgn}(ab) = \operatorname{sgn} a \cdot \operatorname{sgn} b$, so the sign of a product is determined by the signs of the factors — by the kernel theorem this is a congruence. The table: $(-)(-) = (+)$, $(-)(+) = (+)(-) = (-)$, $(+)(+) = (+)$, and $0$ in any product gives $0$.

The identity is $(+)$, the class of the number $1$ (the inheritance theorem). Symmetric elements: $(+) \cdot (+) = (+)$ and $(-) \cdot (-) = (+)$ — both classes are invertible, although the numbers $2$ and $-2$ have no inverses in $(\mathbb{Z}, \cdot)$. The class $0$ is not invertible.

a) Today is Wednesday. What day of the week will it be in $1000$ days? b) Find the remainder of $2^{100}$ divided by $7$ without computing $2^{100}$.

Solution

a) $1000 = 142 \cdot 7 + 6$, so $[1000]_7 = [6]_7$: Wednesday + 6 days = Tuesday.

b) $2^3 = 8 \equiv 1 \pmod 7$. Since the congruence is compatible with multiplication, $2^{99} = (2^3)^{33} \equiv 1^{33} = 1$, and $2^{100} = 2^{99} \cdot 2 \equiv 2 \pmod 7$. The remainder is $2$.

Check the equality $123 \cdot 456 = 56088$ by casting out nines. Will the check catch the error if $56078$ is written as the answer instead? And $56808$?

Solution

$123 \to 6$, $456 \to 15 \to 6$, $6 \cdot 6 = 36 \to 0$ (modulo $9$). $56088 \to 5 + 6 + 0 + 8 + 8 = 27 \to 0$ — it checks out.

$56078 \to 26 \to 8 \ne 0$ — the error is caught. $56808 \to 27 \to 0$ — a transposition of digits does not change the sum, the error is not caught. A check by classes can only refute an answer, not prove it.

On pairs $(a, b)$ with $b \ne 0$ set $(a, b) \sim (a', b') \iff ab' = a'b$. Prove that $\sim$ is a congruence with respect to $(a, b) \oplus (c, d) = (ad + bc,\, bd)$, and that it is not one with respect to the “mediant” $(a + c,\, b + d)$.

Solution

By the “one argument at a time” criterion (the operation is commutative) it is enough to replace the left argument. Let $ab' = a'b$. We need: $(ad + bc)\,b'd = (a'd + b'c)\,bd$. Expand: on the left $ab'd^2 + bb'cd$, on the right $a'bd^2 + bb'cd$. They are equal, since $ab' = a'b$.

The mediant: $(1, 2) \sim (2, 4)$, but $(1, 2) \oplus (1, 3) = (2, 5)$ and $(2, 4) \oplus (1, 3) = (3, 7)$, while $2 \cdot 7 = 14 \ne 15 = 5 \cdot 3$.

Prove that the intersection of two congruences with respect to $\top$ is again a congruence. Is this true for the union? Consider congruence modulo $2$ and modulo $3$ on $\mathbb{Z}$.

Solution

An intersection of equivalences is an equivalence: each of the three properties holds in both relations, hence in the intersection too. If $a \,(R_1 \cap R_2)\, c$ and $b \,(R_1 \cap R_2)\, d$, then since $R_1$ is a congruence the results are $R_1$-equivalent, since $R_2$ is a congruence they are $R_2$-equivalent, that is, equivalent in the intersection.

The union may fail to be even an equivalence: $0 \equiv 2 \pmod 2$, $2 \equiv 5 \pmod 3$, but $0$ and $5$ are congruent neither modulo $2$ nor modulo $3$ — transitivity is violated. (The intersection of these two congruences is congruence modulo $6$.)

Prove that every congruence on $(\mathbb{Z}, +)$ is congruence modulo some $n \geqslant 0$ (for $n = 0$, equality).

Solution

Let $R$ be a congruence, $H = \{x \mid x \,R\, 0\}$. By the “one argument at a time” criterion: $a \,R\, b \Rightarrow (a - b) \,R\, (b - b) = 0$, and conversely, $(a - b) \,R\, 0 \Rightarrow a \,R\, b$ (add $b$). Hence $a \,R\, b \iff a - b \in H$.

$H$ is closed under addition ($x \,R\, 0$, $y \,R\, 0 \Rightarrow x + y \,R\, 0 + 0$) and under taking the opposite ($x \,R\, 0 \Rightarrow (x - x) \,R\, (0 - x)$, that is, $0 \,R\, (-x)$). If $H = \{0\}$, then $R$ is equality. Otherwise let $n$ be the smallest positive number in $H$. All multiples of $n$ lie in $H$. For $x \in H$ divide with remainder: $x = qn + r$, $0 \leqslant r < n$; then $r = x - qn \in H$, and by minimality $r = 0$. Thus $H = n\mathbb{Z}$, and $a \,R\, b \iff n \mid a - b$.

What summary of a collection of numbers is it enough to store so that, from the summaries of two collections, one can find the variance of their union $\sigma^2 = \tfrac1N \sum x_i^2 - \bigl(\tfrac1N \sum x_i\bigr)^2$? Prove that “same variance” is not a congruence for merging.

Solution

The triple $g(X) = \bigl(N, \sum x_i, \sum x_i^2\bigr)$: on merging each component adds up, $g(X \uplus Y) = g(X) + g(Y)$, — by the kernel theorem this is a congruence. The variance is expressed through the triple at the very end.

A counterexample for the variance: $\{0\}$ and $\{5\}$ — both have variance $0$, $\{0\} \,R\, \{5\}$; take also $\{0\} \,R\, \{0\}$. The merges: $\{0, 0\}$ has variance $0$; $\{5, 0\}$ has variance $6.25$.

Summary

  • An equivalence $R$ is a congruence with respect to $\top$ if $a \,R\, c$ and $b \,R\, d$ imply $(a \mathbin{\top} b) \,R\, (c \mathbin{\top} d)$: replace the arguments with equivalent ones — the result is equivalent.
  • Theorem 1.9: for a congruence, the equality $(a/R) \mathbin{\top^*} (b/R) = (a \mathbin{\top} b)/R$ defines an operation on $A/R$ — the answer does not depend on the choice of representatives. The converse is also true: only a congruence gives a well-defined operation.
  • $\top^*$ is the operation associated with $\top$ by $R$ (the quotient operation). It inherits commutativity, associativity, the identity and symmetric elements, and may acquire new properties.
  • It is convenient to check one argument at a time; to refute, one quadruple suffices. For the relation “same summary $f$” the question becomes: can the summary of the result be computed from the summaries of the arguments?
  • The main practical consequence: the class may be taken at any step of a computation — this underlies clocks and calendars, check digits, hashes, cryptography, error-correcting codes and computing metrics over batches.
