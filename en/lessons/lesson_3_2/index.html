Why this matters

In machine learning almost everything is a vector: an image, a text, a purchase history all turn into lists of numbers. And almost everything a model does with them is multiplication by a matrix.

A neural network layer is a weight matrix multiplied by an input vector. Principal component analysis rotates a cloud of data so that its main directions line up with the axes. Computer graphics rotates and stretches objects with the same matrices. To understand these operations, and not just compute them, it helps to see a matrix as a motion of the whole plane.

The main idea of the lesson fits on one line: the columns of a matrix are where the basis vectors go. Everything else follows from it.

Vectors and the basis

A vector in the plane is a pair of numbers $\mathbf{v} = (x, y)$. It is convenient to draw it as an arrow from the origin to the point $(x, y)$. Vectors are added coordinate by coordinate and multiplied by a number:

$$(x_1, y_1) + (x_2, y_2) = (x_1 + x_2,\; y_1 + y_2), \qquad c\,(x, y) = (cx,\; cy).$$

Vectors are covered in detail in ; these two operations are all we need here. Two vectors are special: $\mathbf{e}_1 = (1, 0)$, a step to the right, and $\mathbf{e}_2 = (0, 1)$, a step up. They are called the standard basis, because every vector is built from them:

$$\mathbf{v} = (x, y) = x\,\mathbf{e}_1 + y\,\mathbf{e}_2.$$

The coordinates of a vector are simply “how many steps to the right and how many up.” Remember this way of writing it: later on it will do all the work.

Linear transformations

A transformation of the plane is a rule $T$ that sends each vector $\mathbf{v}$ to a vector $T(\mathbf{v})$. We are interested in the “tidiest” of them.

A transformation $T$ is called linear if for all vectors $\mathbf{u}, \mathbf{v}$ and every number $c$

$$T(\mathbf{u} + \mathbf{v}) = T(\mathbf{u}) + T(\mathbf{v}), \qquad T(c\,\mathbf{v}) = c\,T(\mathbf{v}).$$

In words: it does not matter whether you add the vectors and then transform, or transform and then add; the same goes for scaling. Geometrically this means that the origin stays in place ($T(\mathbf{0}) = T(0 \cdot \mathbf{v}) = 0 \cdot T(\mathbf{v}) = \mathbf{0}$), lines stay lines, and the square grid turns into a grid of identical parallelograms — the lines stay parallel and evenly spaced.

Imagine the plane drawn on a rubber sheet with a square grid. A linear transformation can stretch the sheet, rotate it, skew it or even flatten it into a line — but only “uniformly”: all the cells change in the same way, and the nail at the origin does not move.

A matrix is the images of the basis

Apply $T$ to the expression and use linearity:

$$T(\mathbf{v}) = T(x\,\mathbf{e}_1 + y\,\mathbf{e}_2) = x\,T(\mathbf{e}_1) + y\,T(\mathbf{e}_2).$$

This is the main result of the lesson. To know where a linear transformation sends any vector, it is enough to know where it sends the two basis vectors. Write their images as columns and you get a table of four numbers, a matrix:

If $T(\mathbf{e}_1) = (a, c)$ and $T(\mathbf{e}_2) = (b, d)$, then for every vector $(x, y)$

$$T\begin{pmatrix} x \\ y \end{pmatrix} = \underbrace{\begin{pmatrix} a & b \\ c & d \end{pmatrix}}_{A}\begin{pmatrix} x \\ y \end{pmatrix} = x\begin{pmatrix} a \\ c \end{pmatrix} + y\begin{pmatrix} b \\ d \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} ax + by \\ cx + dy \end{pmatrix}.$$

The first column of $A$ is the image of $\mathbf{e}_1$, the second is the image of $\mathbf{e}_2$.

Change the entries of the matrix or pick a preset transformation. The blue arrow is the first column ($A\mathbf{e}_1$), the orange one is the second ($A\mathbf{e}_2$). The shaded region is the image of the unit square. You can drag the vector $\mathbf{v}$ with the mouse — the black arrow shows $A\mathbf{v}$. The “blend” slider runs the transition from the identity transformation to $A$.

Try it in the figure: choose “Shear” — the first column $(1, 0)$ stays in place, while the second moves to $(1, 1)$. The whole grid is skewed, but horizontal lines stay horizontal. Choose “Projection” — the second column is zero, and the whole plane lies down on the $x$-axis.

Multiplying a matrix by a vector

Formula gives two equally valid ways to compute $A\mathbf{v}$.

Take $A = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}$ and $\mathbf{v} = (1, 2)$.

By columns — “one step along the first column, two steps along the second”:

$$A\mathbf{v} = 1\begin{pmatrix} 2 \\ 0 \end{pmatrix} + 2\begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 4 \\ 2 \end{pmatrix}.$$

By rows — each coordinate of the result is the sum of the pairwise products of a row with the vector:

$$A\mathbf{v} = \begin{pmatrix} 2\cdot 1 + 1 \cdot 2 \\ 0 \cdot 1 + 1 \cdot 2 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 4 \\ 2 \end{pmatrix}.$$

The “by rows” way is convenient for computing by hand and in code. The “by columns” way explains the meaning: the result is a combination of the columns with coefficients taken from the vector. This is exactly the matrix the figure opens with by default: move $\mathbf{v}$ to the point $(1, 2)$ and check that $A\mathbf{v}$ lands at $(4, 2)$.

A neural network layer $\mathbf{h} = W\mathbf{x}$ works the same way, only the matrix $W$ is large: for example, $512 \times 784$. Each row of $W$ is a “detector” that compares the input with its own template of weights, and each column is the contribution of one input feature to all the outputs.

A gallery of transformations

Once you know the rule “columns = images of the basis,” you can write down the matrix of any transformation without computing anything: you only need to picture where $\mathbf{e}_1$ and $\mathbf{e}_2$ will go.

Under a counterclockwise rotation by an angle $\varphi$, the vector $\mathbf{e}_1 = (1, 0)$ goes to $(\cos\varphi, \sin\varphi)$ — this is the definition of cosine and sine on the unit circle. The vector $\mathbf{e}_2 = (0, 1)$ is $90^\circ$ ahead: it goes to $(-\sin\varphi, \cos\varphi)$. Therefore,

$$R_\varphi = \begin{pmatrix} \cos\varphi & -\sin\varphi \\ \sin\varphi & \phantom{-}\cos\varphi \end{pmatrix}.$$

The last column of the table is the determinant. Let us now figure out what it measures.

The determinant: how much area changes

The unit square with sides $\mathbf{e}_1$ and $\mathbf{e}_2$ has area $1$. The matrix maps it to a parallelogram whose sides are the columns $(a, c)$ and $(b, d)$ — it is shaded in the figure. Since all the cells of the grid change in the same way, the area of any figure changes by the same factor.

The determinant of the matrix $A = \begin{pmatrix} a & b \\ c & d \end{pmatrix}$ is the number

$$\det A = ad - bc.$$

The area of the parallelogram spanned by the columns $(a, c)$ and $(b, d)$ equals $|\det A| = |ad - bc|$. The sign of the determinant shows the orientation: when $\det A > 0$, the turn from the first column to the second is counterclockwise, as from $\mathbf{e}_1$ to $\mathbf{e}_2$; when $\det A < 0$, it is clockwise, and the plane is “flipped over.”

Proof for the typical case $a, b, c, d > 0$, $ad > bc$

Inscribe the parallelogram in a rectangle with sides $a + b$ and $c + d$: its vertices are $\mathbf{0}$, $(a, c)$, $(a+b, c+d)$, $(b, d)$. Outside the parallelogram there remain two triangles with legs $a$ and $c$, two with legs $b$ and $d$, and two $b \times c$ rectangles in the corners. Subtract:

$$S = (a+b)(c+d) - 2\cdot\tfrac{1}{2}ac - 2\cdot\tfrac{1}{2}bd - 2bc = ad - bc.$$

The other arrangements of the columns are handled in the same way or reduced to this one by rotations, which change neither the area nor the sign, and reflections, which change only the sign. The general proof is in .

Let us check this on the matrix from the example: $\det\begin{pmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix} = 2\cdot 1 - 1\cdot 0 = 2$ — the area doubles. For a rotation, $\det R_\varphi = \cos^2\varphi + \sin^2\varphi = 1$: the area does not change, as expected. And for a shear it is also $1$ — skewing does not change area, just like a deck of cards pushed sideways.

When the determinant is zero

$\det A = 0$ means that the square has turned into a figure of zero area — a segment or a point. The columns lie on one line, and the whole plane is flattened onto that line (or onto a point). Such a transformation cannot be inverted: different vectors have been glued into one, and from the result you can no longer recover where they came from. A matrix with zero determinant is called singular.

In machine learning, singularity is a common source of trouble. If two features in the data are linearly dependent (for example, a price in rubles and the same price in dollars), the matrix in the linear regression formula becomes singular, and there is no unique solution. This is exactly what regularization fixes.

Common mistakes

The image of $\mathbf{e}_1$ is the first column $(a, c)$, not the first row $(a, b)$. This is especially visible for a rotation by $90^\circ$: the first column $(0, 1)$ is correct — $\mathbf{e}_1$ went up; the first row $(0, -1)$ would point down.

The determinant is not linear in the matrix as a whole: $\det(I + I) = \det(2I) = 4$, whereas $\det I + \det I = 2$. When you stretch by a factor of 2 along both axes, the area grows by a factor of 4.

The projection $\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix}$ is nonzero but singular. A zero determinant tells you about flattening, not that everything has become zero.

Problems

For $A = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 3 & 4 \end{pmatrix}$ and $\mathbf{v} = (2, -1)$, find $A\mathbf{v}$ in two ways and compute $\det A$. Does $A$ preserve orientation?

Solution

By columns: $2\,(1, 3) - 1\,(2, 4) = (0, 2)$. By rows: $(1\cdot 2 + 2\cdot(-1),\; 3\cdot 2 + 4\cdot(-1)) = (0, 2)$.

$\det A = 1\cdot 4 - 2\cdot 3 = -2$: the area doubles, and the orientation is reversed.

Without using formula , write the matrix of a counterclockwise rotation by $90^\circ$, apply it to $(3, 1)$ and check that the length of the vector has not changed.

Solution

$\mathbf{e}_1 = (1, 0)$ goes up, to $(0, 1)$; $\mathbf{e}_2 = (0, 1)$ goes left, to $(-1, 0)$. The columns: $\begin{pmatrix} 0 & -1 \\ 1 & 0 \end{pmatrix}$.

$3\,(0, 1) + 1\,(-1, 0) = (-1, 3)$. The lengths: $\sqrt{3^2 + 1^2} = \sqrt{10}$ and $\sqrt{(-1)^2 + 3^2} = \sqrt{10}$ — they are equal.

Prove: if $\det A = 0$, then there is a nonzero vector $\mathbf{v}$ for which $A\mathbf{v} = \mathbf{0}$. Why does it follow that $A$ cannot be inverted?

Solution

Let $ad = bc$. Take $\mathbf{v} = (d, -c)$: $A\mathbf{v} = (ad - bc,\; cd - dc) = (0, 0)$. If $c = d = 0$, take $\mathbf{v} = (b, -a)$: $A\mathbf{v} = (ab - ba,\; 0) = (0, 0)$. And if all four numbers are zero, any $\mathbf{v} \ne \mathbf{0}$ will do.

Then $A\mathbf{v} = A\mathbf{0} = \mathbf{0}$ with $\mathbf{v} \ne \mathbf{0}$: two different vectors are mapped to one, and the inverse transformation cannot know which of them to return.

Summary

  • A linear transformation preserves addition and multiplication by a number: the origin stays in place, and the grid is made of identical parallelograms.
  • It is completely determined by the images of $\mathbf{e}_1$ and $\mathbf{e}_2$; written as columns, they are the matrix.
  • $A\mathbf{v}$ is a combination of the columns of $A$ with coefficients from $\mathbf{v}$; by rows, it is the same vector computed differently.
  • $\det A = ad - bc$ is the factor by which area changes; its sign tells whether orientation is preserved.
  • $\det A = 0$: the plane is flattened, and there is no inverse transformation.
