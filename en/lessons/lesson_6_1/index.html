Why this matters

To train a model means to find the parameters at which the loss function $L$ is minimal. There are thousands or billions of parameters, and there is no formula for the minimum. All that is left is to walk toward it step by step.

In we saw that at a smooth minimum the derivative equals zero. For a function of one variable, the equation $f'(x) = 0$ can sometimes be solved on paper. But for a neural network, $L$ depends on millions of numbers at once, and there are just as many equations — solving them explicitly is impossible. Gradient descent sidesteps the problem: it does not look for the minimum with a formula but approaches it step by step, each time asking the derivative which way to go.

This algorithm trains linear and logistic regression, neural networks and — in a slightly disguised form — gradient boosting.

Walking down a mountain in the fog

Imagine you are standing on a mountainside in thick fog and want to get down into the valley. You cannot see the valley — only the ground under your feet. A sensible strategy: feel out where the slope goes down most steeply, take a step that way, and repeat.

The height is the loss function, your position is the model's parameters, the steepness of the slope under your feet is the derivative (on a plane, the gradient), and the length of a step is the learning rate. The whole algorithm is “step down the slope, repeat.”

This picture already contains the three questions of the lesson: how to “feel out the slope” mathematically, how long a step to take, and whether this way you always reach the very lowest point.

A step in one dimension

Let us start with a function of one variable $f(x)$. The derivative $f'(x)$ is the slope of the tangent line: if $f'(x) > 0$, the function increases to the right, so we should descend to the left; if $f'(x) < 0$, the other way round. In both cases we must move against the sign of the derivative. That is exactly how a step works:

Choose a starting point $x_0$ and a number $\eta > 0$, the learning rate. A step of gradient descent is

$$x_{t+1} = x_t - \eta\, f'(x_t), \qquad t = 0, 1, 2, \ldots$$
$x_t$
the position after $t$ steps
$f'(x_t)$
the slope at the current point: both the direction and the “strength” of the step — on a steep slope the step is longer
$\eta$
the learning rate, a multiplier of the step length; it is chosen by a human

Why does the step really lead downhill? The answer comes from the linear approximation from the lesson on the derivative: $f(x + h) \approx f(x) + f'(x)\,h$.

If $f$ is differentiable at the point $x$ and $f'(x) \ne 0$, then for all sufficiently small $\eta > 0$ we have $f\big(x - \eta f'(x)\big) < f(x)$.

Proof

Let $h = -\eta f'(x)$. By the definition of the derivative, the difference quotient $\frac{f(x+h) - f(x)}{h}$ tends to $f'(x)$ as $h \to 0$, that is, it equals $f'(x) + \alpha$, where $\alpha \to 0$. Then

$$f(x+h) - f(x) = \big(f'(x) + \alpha\big)\,h = -\eta\, f'(x)^2 - \eta\,\alpha\, f'(x).$$

The first term is negative and equals $-\eta f'(x)^2$. The absolute value of the second is $\eta\,|\alpha|\,|f'(x)|$, and for small $\eta$ (and hence small $h$) the factor $|\alpha|$ becomes smaller than $|f'(x)|$. Then the second term is smaller than the first in absolute value, and the sum is negative.

Let us compute by hand. Let $f(x) = x^2$; then $f'(x) = 2x$. Take $x_0 = 3$ and $\eta = 0.1$:

The learning rate

For $f(x) = x^2$ the step can be written in closed form: $x_{t+1} = x_t - 2\eta\,x_t = (1 - 2\eta)\,x_t$, and therefore

$$x_t = (1 - 2\eta)^t\, x_0.$$

The whole fate of the descent is determined by a single number, the multiplier $q = 1 - 2\eta$. If $|q| < 1$, the powers $q^t$ tend to zero and the descent converges; if $|q| > 1$, it blows up.

In gradient boosting the same role is played by the learning rate $\nu$ (shrinkage): each new tree is added with the multiplier $\nu$, and small values of $\nu$ require more trees. For details, see the course “Gradient Boosting, Bone by Bone,” lesson “Where gradient descent is hidden”.

Partial derivatives and the gradient

A model has not one parameter but many. For now, let there be two: the function $f(x, y)$ is a landscape over the plane, the height at each point $(x, y)$. How do we measure the slope if we can walk in any direction?

Let us start with the two simplest directions — along the axes. Fix $y$ and vary only $x$: we get an ordinary function of one variable, and it has an ordinary derivative.

The partial derivative of a function $f(x, y)$ with respect to $x$ is the derivative with respect to $x$ with $y$ held fixed:

$$\frac{\partial f}{\partial x}(x, y) = \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{f(x + h,\, y) - f(x, y)}{h}.$$

The partial derivative with respect to $y$ is defined in the same way, with $x$ held fixed.

The gradient of a function $f(x, y)$ is the vector of its partial derivatives:

$$\nabla f(x, y) = \left(\frac{\partial f}{\partial x},\; \frac{\partial f}{\partial y}\right).$$

Let $f(x, y) = x^2 + 5y^2$. With $y$ fixed, the term $5y^2$ is a constant, and its derivative is zero, so $\frac{\partial f}{\partial x} = 2x$. Similarly, $\frac{\partial f}{\partial y} = 10y$. Thus $\nabla f(x, y) = (2x,\; 10y)$; for example, at the point $(1, 2)$ the gradient equals $(2, 20)$.

The symbol $\nabla$ is read “nabla.” For a function of $n$ variables, the gradient is the vector of $n$ partial derivatives; everything that follows carries over to it without change.

Why the gradient

The gradient is assembled from the slopes along the axes. But why does it point in the direction of the steepest ascent rather than just some direction? Suppose we move from the point $(x, y)$ in the direction of a unit vector $\mathbf{u} = (u_1, u_2)$, $u_1^2 + u_2^2 = 1$. The slope in this direction, the directional derivative, equals, by the linear approximation,

$$D_{\mathbf{u}} f = \frac{\partial f}{\partial x}\,u_1 + \frac{\partial f}{\partial y}\,u_2 = \nabla f \cdot \mathbf{u}.$$

On the right is the dot product — the sum of the pairwise products of the coordinates. It has a geometric meaning, which we will examine in detail in : $\mathbf{a} \cdot \mathbf{b} = |\mathbf{a}|\,|\mathbf{b}|\cos\varphi$, where $\varphi$ is the angle between the vectors.

If $\nabla f(x, y) \ne \mathbf{0}$, then among all unit directions $\mathbf{u}$ the directional derivative $D_{\mathbf{u}} f$ is largest for $\mathbf{u} = \nabla f / |\nabla f|$ and smallest for $\mathbf{u} = -\nabla f / |\nabla f|$. Its values there are $\pm|\nabla f|$.

Proof

By formula and the geometric meaning of the dot product, $D_{\mathbf{u}} f = |\nabla f|\cdot 1 \cdot \cos\varphi$, where $\varphi$ is the angle between $\nabla f$ and $\mathbf{u}$. The cosine is maximal ($= 1$) at $\varphi = 0$, that is, when $\mathbf{u}$ points the same way as the gradient, and minimal ($= -1$) at $\varphi = 180^\circ$.

This yields one more consequence. A level curve is a curve on which $f$ is constant, like a contour line on a topographic map. Moving along it, the height does not change, so $D_{\mathbf{u}} f = 0$ and $\cos\varphi = 0$: the gradient is perpendicular to the level curves. On the map this is immediately visible — the gradient arrow always crosses the contour lines at a right angle.

Descent on the plane

Now the gradient descent step for many variables is written the same way as for one, only with the gradient instead of the derivative:

$$\boldsymbol{\theta}_{t+1} = \boldsymbol{\theta}_t - \eta\, \nabla f(\boldsymbol{\theta}_t).$$

Here $\boldsymbol{\theta} = (x, y)$ is a point on the plane, and for a real model it is the vector of all parameters. This is exactly the formula on the course's home page.

The ravine: why one rate for all directions is a compromise

For $f = x^2 + 5y^2$ the step splits into two independent ones: $x_{t+1} = (1 - 2\eta)\,x_t$ and $y_{t+1} = (1 - 10\eta)\,y_t$. The descent converges only if both multipliers are less than one in absolute value, that is, for $\eta < 0.2$ — the threshold is set by the steepest direction. But along the gentle direction $x$, with such an $\eta$, the steps are small.

Hence the familiar picture: at $\eta = 0.18$ the multiplier along $y$ is $1 - 1.8 = -0.8$ — the descent bounces from one wall of the ravine to the other, while along $x$ (multiplier $0.64$) it slowly creeps along the bottom. At $\eta = 0.21$ the multiplier along $y$ equals $-1.1$, and the descent flies out of the ravine. This effect is the main reason momentum methods and adaptive methods such as Adam were invented.

Local minima

Gradient descent sees only the slope under its feet. If a function has several wells, it rolls into the one on whose slope it started — and never learns that somewhere there is a deeper well.

The function $f(x) = x^4 - 3x^2 + x$ has two minima: near $x \approx -1.30$ (where $f \approx -3.51$) and near $x \approx 1.13$ (where $f \approx -1.07$). The descent from $x_0 = -0.5$ rolls into the deep left well, and from $x_0 = 0.4$ into the shallow right one: between them is a hump at the point $x \approx 0.17$. Check this in the first figure by choosing the function “two wells.”

A point where the function is smaller than at all nearby points is called a local minimum; the lowest point overall is called the global minimum. There are functions for which every local minimum is global — convex functions; they are the subject of . The loss functions of linear and logistic regression are convex, those of neural networks are not, and nevertheless descent with a good initialization finds good solutions for them.

Common mistakes

The gradient points to where the function increases. Minimization needs a minus sign: $\boldsymbol\theta - \eta\nabla f$. A plus gives gradient ascent — it is also sometimes needed, for example when maximizing the likelihood.

Up to the threshold, increasing $\eta$ speeds up the descent; beyond it, it leads to oscillations and divergence. If the training loss grows or “jumps around,” the first thing to do is to lower the learning rate.

The descent approaches the minimum for infinitely long: $|q|^t$ never becomes zero. In practice one stops when $|\nabla f|$ or the change in the loss becomes smaller than a threshold, or after a set number of steps.

Problems

For $f(x) = (x - 2)^2$, $\eta = 0.25$, $x_0 = 0$, take two steps of gradient descent. Find a formula for $x_t$.

Solution

$f'(x) = 2(x - 2)$. Step: $x_{t+1} = x_t - 0.5(x_t - 2)$. $x_1 = 0 - 0.5\cdot(-2) = 1$, $x_2 = 1 - 0.5\cdot(-1) = 1.5$.

The distance to the minimum, $x_t - 2$, is multiplied by $0.5$ each time: $x_t = 2 - 2\cdot 0.5^t$.

Find the gradient of the function $f(x, y) = x^2 y + y^3$ at the point $(1, 2)$. In which direction from this point does the function decrease fastest?

Solution

$\frac{\partial f}{\partial x} = 2xy$, $\frac{\partial f}{\partial y} = x^2 + 3y^2$. At the point $(1, 2)$: $\nabla f = (4,\; 13)$.

The function decreases fastest in the direction $-\nabla f = (-4, -13)$, normalized: $(-4, -13)/\sqrt{185}$.

Let $f(x) = \frac{a}{2}x^2$, $a > 0$. For which $\eta$ does gradient descent converge to the minimum from any starting point? For which $\eta$ does it get there in one step?

Solution

$f'(x) = ax$, the step is $x_{t+1} = (1 - \eta a)\,x_t$. Convergence holds when $|1 - \eta a| < 1$, that is, $0 < \eta < 2/a$. In one step — when $1 - \eta a = 0$, $\eta = 1/a$.

The steeper the parabola (the larger $a$, the second derivative), the smaller the admissible rate. For $x^2$ ($a = 2$) we get the threshold $1$ and the ideal rate $0.5$ — as in the table above.

Summary

  • The step $x_{t+1} = x_t - \eta f'(x_t)$ goes against the slope; for small $\eta$ it decreases the function — this follows from the linear approximation.
  • The learning rate decides everything: too little — it creeps, just right — it converges, too much — it oscillates and blows up. For $\frac{a}{2}x^2$ the threshold is $2/a$.
  • A partial derivative is an ordinary derivative with respect to one variable with the others held fixed; the gradient is the vector of them.
  • The gradient is the direction of steepest ascent, perpendicular to the level curves; descent goes against it: $\boldsymbol\theta_{t+1} = \boldsymbol\theta_t - \eta\nabla f(\boldsymbol\theta_t)$.
  • In ravines one rate for all directions is a compromise; with several wells the result depends on the start.
